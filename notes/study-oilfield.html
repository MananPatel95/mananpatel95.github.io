The big picture

Upstream oil is a series of bets on rock you can't see. Each step costs more than the one before and removes some uncertainty, until a company is confident enough to spend billions.

On land (US shale)

The oil is known to be there. The game is manufacturing: drill thousands of near-identical horizontal wells cheaply and fast. Weeks per well, millions per well.

At sea (deepwater)

The oil is a big unknown until you drill. The game is megaprojects: a few huge fields, a floating factory, decades of output. Years per project, billions per project.

Phase 1

Why oil is where it is

Oil is ancient plankton and algae buried in mud, cooked by heat and pressure over millions of years. You need a petroleum system, where all the pieces line up:

  • Source rock: organic-rich shale that generated the oil.
  • Migration: oil is lighter than water, so it moves upward through rock.
  • Reservoir: porous, permeable rock (sandstone, limestone) that holds it like a sponge.
  • Trap + seal: a shape (dome, fault) capped by tight rock that stops it escaping.
  • Timing: the trap had to exist before the oil migrated.

Conventional

Oil migrated into a trap. Drill into the trap and it flows. Classic offshore and Middle East fields.

Unconventional (shale)

Drill into the source rock itself. It's too tight to flow, so you crack it open by fracking. This is the Permian, Bakken, Eagle Ford.

Answer it cold

Read: PetroWiki "Petroleum system". AAPG "Petroleum geology" primer. Yergin, The Prize, for the history.

Phase 2

Rights & leasing

Before you touch the ground you need the right to drill and keep what comes out. The US is unusual: private people can own the minerals under their land. Almost everywhere else, the state owns them.

On land (US)

A landman finds the mineral owner and negotiates a lease: an upfront bonus per acre, a royalty share of production, and a primary term (say 3–5 years). Drill and produce before it ends and the lease is "held by production."

At sea

Governments run lease sales or licensing rounds (BOEM in the US Gulf). Abroad it's often a production sharing contract (PSC): the company pays the costs, then recovers them and splits "profit oil" with the state. Guyana is the famous recent case.

Numbers to know
  • Royalties typically run 12.5%–25% of production value. Many private Texas leases sit at 20–25%.
  • Texas also charges severance tax on production (about 4.6% on oil).
  • Mineral rights get split, sold and inherited, so one well can have hundreds of royalty owners.

Answer it cold

Read: Texas Pacific Land or Viper Energy 10-K (business section). BOEM lease sale overview. A plain-English explainer on Guyana's PSC with ExxonMobil.

Phase 3

Seismic survey

Seismic is ultrasound for the earth. You send sound down, and it bounces back off each rock layer at different times. Millions of these echoes become a 2D slice or a 3D "cube" of the subsurface.

On land

Vibroseis trucks shake the ground with a heavy plate (or small dynamite charges where trucks can't go). Lines of geophones listen. Crews negotiate access with every landowner.

At sea

A boat fires air guns and tows streamers kilometres long, full of hydrophones. Ocean-bottom nodes laid on the seafloor give better pictures at a higher cost.

The business model twist: seismic companies often shoot multi-client surveys on their own dime, then license the same data to many oil companies. Shoot once, sell many times. That's a data business, not a services business.

Answer it cold

Read: SEG Wiki "Seismic reflection". TGS annual report (multi-client model). Watch a vibroseis crew video and a marine streamer deployment video.

Phase 4

Interpretation & the prospect

Geophysicists and geologists pick horizons and faults in the seismic cube, map possible traps and estimate how much oil one might hold. A mapped, drillable trap is a prospect. Each one gets a chance of success by multiplying the odds that every part of the petroleum system works.

Numbers to know
  • A frontier "wildcat" often has a success chance around 10–30%. Near existing fields it's much higher.
  • Prospect size is a range, not a number: P90 (low case), P50 (mid), P10 (high).
  • Expected value ≈ chance of success × value if found − cost of a dry hole.

Answer it cold

Read: PetroWiki "Prospect risk analysis". SLB Petrel product pages (what interpreters actually use).

Phase 5

Exploration well

The only way to know is to drill. The first well into a new prospect is the wildcat. While drilling, tools measure the rock (well logs: resistivity, porosity, gamma ray), and cores or fluid samples may come up. Result: a dry hole or a discovery.

On land

A land rig on trucks, a few weeks of drilling. In shale basins true wildcats are rare now because the rock is mapped, so the risk is cost and productivity, not "is it there."

At sea

Shallow water: a jackup (legs on the seabed). Deep water: a semisubmersible or drillship held in place by thrusters. A deepwater wildcat can cost $100M+.

Answer it cold

Read: Transocean or Valaris fleet pages and 10-K. PetroWiki "Well logging". The Liza discovery story (Guyana, 2015).

Phase 6

Appraisal & reserves

One discovery well tells you oil exists, not how much. Appraisal wells and flow tests measure the field's extent, thickness and how fast it flows. Then the oil gets classified into reserves: the recoverable, economic, booked part.

The ladder
  • Resources: oil that's there, whether or not you can profitably get it.
  • Reserves: what you can produce profitably under current conditions. Proved (1P), Proved + Probable (2P), + Possible (3P).
  • US public companies report proved reserves under SEC rules, often audited by independent firms. PV-10 is their discounted value at 10%.

Answer it cold

Read: SPE PRMS summary. The reserves section of any E&P 10-K (search "proved reserves"). Ryder Scott's website for what a reserve auditor does.

Phase 7

The development decision (FID)

Final investment decision is the moment a company and its partners commit the big money. Engineers pick a development concept, finance models the cash flows, and the board says yes or no.

On land

FID is a pad of wells, a few tens of millions. It's repeated constantly, almost like a factory deciding next quarter's output. Key number: breakeven oil price per well.

At sea

Pick a concept: fixed platform, spar, TLP, FPSO (floating production ship) or a subsea tieback to existing infrastructure. Billions up front, first oil years later, decades of production.

The model
  • Inputs: production profile × oil price − royalty − taxes − operating cost − capex.
  • Outputs: NPV (usually at a 10% discount rate), IRR, payback period, breakeven price.
  • Industry surveys (Dallas Fed Energy Survey) put new US shale well breakevens somewhere around $60–70 WTI. Check the latest.

Answer it cold

Read: Dallas Fed Energy Survey breakeven questions. An Exxon Guyana project page (Yellowtail or Hammerhead). SBM Offshore annual report (FPSO lease model).

Phase 8

Drilling & completing wells

A well is a telescope of steel pipe. Drill a section, run casing, cement it, drill a smaller section below, repeat. Drilling mud cools the bit, carries rock chips up and holds back pressure. A blowout preventer sits on top as the last line of defence.

On land (shale)

Drill down about 2 km, turn sideways and drill a horizontal lateral that can run 2–3 miles. Then complete it: perforate, and frack in dozens of stages with water, sand and chemicals to crack the rock open. Many wells per pad.

At sea

Wells end in subsea trees (valve stacks on the seafloor), connected by flowlines and risers up to the FPSO or platform. Installation vessels, ROVs and specialist subsea contractors do this work.

Numbers to know
  • A Permian horizontal well costs roughly $6–10M all-in, and drilling plus completion take weeks.
  • Land rig day rates are tens of thousands of dollars; deepwater drillships several hundred thousand.
  • Completion (fracking) is often more than half the well's cost.

Answer it cold

Read: Halliburton or Liberty Energy 10-K (completions). Helmerich & Payne 10-K (land rigs). TechnipFMC subsea overview. Watch a "life of a frac" animation.

Phase 9

First oil & decline

Every well produces most at the start, then declines as pressure drops. Plotting that is the type curve, the most important chart in a shale investor deck.

On land

Shale wells can fall about 60–70% in year one. To stay flat a company has to keep drilling, which is the "treadmill." When prices fall, US output follows within months.

At sea

Big fields plateau for years, then decline slowly. Water or gas injection keeps pressure up. Output responds to price over years, not months.

After production: oil flows to midstream (gathering, pipelines), which is where the existing Tiny Oil & Gas takes over. At the end of field life comes plugging and abandonment (offshore decommissioning is a big, costly business of its own).

Answer it cold

Read: Diamondback or EOG investor presentation (find the type curves). EIA "Drilling Productivity" data.

Boom vs bust

When oil goes up, day rates and service margins jump first, operators make windfalls, and royalty owners collect more without spending anything. When oil crashes, drillers and service companies get hit hardest (rigs stacked, crews laid off), leveraged operators face borrowing-base cuts, and royalty owners just get a smaller check. Know 2014–16 and 2020 as the two case studies.

Glossary

BasinA large low area where sediment piled up over millions of years. Oil only forms in these.
WildcatAn exploration well in an unproven area.
Working interestOwnership share that pays its share of costs and gets its share of production.
Farm-outGiving part of your stake to a partner in exchange for them paying for drilling.
LateralThe horizontal section of a shale well.
CompletionMaking a drilled well ready to produce. In shale, mostly fracking.
FPSOFloating production, storage and offloading vessel.
TiebackConnecting new subsea wells to an existing platform instead of building a new one.
EUREstimated ultimate recovery: total a well will produce over its life.
PV-10Present value of proved reserves at a 10% discount rate (SEC standard).
Borrowing baseHow much a bank will lend against your reserves; reset twice a year.
P&APlug and abandon: permanently sealing a well at end of life.
