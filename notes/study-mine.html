The big picture

Mining is a funnel even steeper than oil's. Thousands of prospects get looked at, a handful become deposits and very few become mines, often 10–20 years after discovery. Along the way the project usually changes hands, and its value is set on a stock exchange long before any metal is sold.

Junior explorers

Small listed companies with no revenue. They raise money, drill and hope to find something a major will buy. Most fail; a few make investors 10–100×.

Majors

BHP, Rio Tinto, Newmont, Freeport. They run mines, buy discoveries and replace depleting reserves. They explore less themselves than you'd think.

Phase 1

Why metal is where it is

Ore deposits are rare places where geology concentrated a metal hundreds or thousands of times above normal levels, usually via hot fluids moving through cracks in rock. Different processes make different deposit types, and explorers hunt by type.

  • Porphyry copper: huge, low-grade, near old volcanoes. Most of the world's copper (Chile, Arizona).
  • Orogenic and epithermal gold: veins along faults or near hot springs. Higher grade, smaller.
  • VMS: copper-zinc deposits formed at ancient seafloor vents.
  • Lithium: hard-rock pegmatites (Australia) or brines pumped from salt flats (Chile, Argentina).
  • Placer: gold eroded and concentrated in rivers. The original gold rushes.

Answer it cold

Read: USGS mineral deposit models overviews. Visual Capitalist pieces on copper and lithium supply.

Phase 2

Grassroots exploration

Before drilling, explorers narrow thousands of square kilometres down to a few targets, cheaply.

  • Mapping & sampling: geologists walk the ground, break rocks, take soil and stream-sediment samples.
  • Airborne geophysics: planes or drones measure magnetism, conductivity (EM) and radioactivity from the air.
  • Ground geophysics: IP (induced polarization) surveys find disseminated sulphides, which is classic for porphyry copper.
  • Remote sensing: satellite hyperspectral images spot altered minerals at the surface.
  • Data: old government surveys and historic drill holes, increasingly mined with ML.

Answer it cold

Read: KoBold Metals coverage (Mingomba copper discovery). A junior's "exploration program" news release on SEDAR+ or its website.

Phase 3

Staking a claim

In most places the state owns minerals and grants mineral tenure: a claim or exploration licence, then later a mining lease. Many jurisdictions now let you stake claims online. Holding claims requires fees or a minimum amount of work each year.

Option deals are common here: a prospector or "prospect generator" lets a company earn into the property by spending money on it and paying cash and shares, while keeping a royalty.

Answer it cold

Read: Fraser Institute Annual Survey of Mining Companies (jurisdiction rankings). EMX Royalty's business overview.

Phase 4

Drilling & assay

Now you drill to sample the rock at depth. RC drilling (reverse circulation) is cheaper and returns rock chips; diamond drilling returns intact core, which geologists log and photograph. Core is cut in half: one half goes to the lab, one half is kept.

At the lab, assay measures how much metal is in each sample. Gold usually uses fire assay (melt the sample, collect the gold in lead, weigh it). Base metals use acid digestion + ICP (instrument analysis). Every batch includes QA/QC: blanks, certified standards and duplicates, to prove the lab and the samples are honest.

Reading a drill result
  • "12 m @ 4.2 g/t Au" means 12 metres of core averaging 4.2 grams of gold per tonne of rock.
  • Multiply for "gram-metres" (≈50 here) as a quick size signal.
  • Check if it's drilled width or true width, and whether a short high-grade bit is propping up a long average.
  • For context, 1 g/t ≈ 1 part per million. Big open-pit gold mines can run around 1 g/t.

Cautionary tale: Bre-X (1990s) salted its samples with outside gold and became a multi-billion-dollar fraud. It's why QA/QC and Qualified Person rules exist.

Answer it cold

Read: ALS Global or SGS service pages (fire assay). Major Drilling annual report. Any junior's drill-results news release. A Bre-X documentary or article.

Phase 5

Resource estimate

Geologists take all the drill holes and build a 3D block model: the deposit chopped into blocks, each with an estimated grade (using geostatistics like kriging). A Qualified Person (QP) signs off on it under disclosure rules.

The confidence ladder
  • Inferred → Indicated → Measured resources: more drilling = more confidence.
  • Reserves (Probable, Proven) only exist after a pre-feasibility or feasibility study proves they're economic to mine.
  • Rules: NI 43-101 (Canada), JORC (Australia), S-K 1300 (US SEC).

Answer it cold

Read: CIM Definition Standards. Seequent Leapfrog product demos (block models). The resource section of one NI 43-101 report.

Phase 6

The studies ladder

Each study costs more and narrows the uncertainty on cost and value. It's the mining twin of oil's FID process.

StudyAccuracy (rough)What it's for
PEA / scoping±30–50%"Is this worth more work?" Can include inferred resources (with warnings).
Pre-feasibility (PFS)±20–25%Pick the mining and processing method. First reserves.
Feasibility (FS / DFS)±10–15%Bankable: what lenders and boards decide on.
Metrics in every study
  • NPV (often at 5% for gold, 8% for base metals) and IRR.
  • Initial capex, payback, life of mine (LOM).
  • AISC (all-in sustaining cost) per ounce or per pound.
  • Strip ratio (waste rock per tonne of ore), head grade, recovery %.

Answer it cold

Read: one NI 43-101 feasibility study on SEDAR+ (sections 1 and 22). World Gold Council AISC guidance.

Phase 7

Rock to stock: financing

This is the part most people never see. A junior with no revenue funds years of drilling and studies by selling shares, and the stock price tracks the project's risk.

  • Listing: most juniors list on the TSX Venture, ASX or LSE AIM. Toronto hosts more listed mining companies than any other exchange.
  • Private placements: sell new shares (often with warrants) to funds and retail investors via mining-focused brokers. Canada adds flow-through shares, which pass tax deductions to investors.
  • Dilution: every raise shrinks existing owners' stake, so share count matters.
  • Streams and royalties: a company like Franco-Nevada or Wheaton pays cash up front for a cut of future production or metal at a fixed low price.
  • Debt and offtake: project-finance banks and export credit agencies lend after a feasibility study. Traders (Glencore, Trafigura) may prepay for future concentrate.
  • The exit: often a takeover by a major or mid-tier, rather than building the mine.
The Lassonde Curve
  • Stock rises on discovery (speculation), peaks around the resource estimate.
  • Falls through studies, permitting and financing (the "orphan period": early speculators leave, producers' investors aren't in yet).
  • Rises again on construction and production.

Answer it cold

Read: Franco-Nevada or Wheaton Precious Metals annual report. TMX "Mining" listing stats. Search "Lassonde Curve" for the original chart.

Phase 8

Permits & construction

Permitting can take longer than all the geology. Environmental impact assessments, water and air permits, and agreements with Indigenous nations and local communities (impact-benefit agreements) all have to land. Then an EPCM firm engineers and manages construction of the pit or shaft, the mill, tailings facility, power, water and roads.

Open pit

Big, shallow, low-grade deposits. Blast benches, load with shovels, haul with 300-tonne trucks. Cheap per tonne, with lots of waste rock.

Underground

Deeper, higher-grade deposits. Shafts or ramps, drilling and blasting stopes, then backfilling. More expensive per tonne, less surface disturbance.

Answer it cold

Read: S&P Global research on mine lead times. Resolution Copper (Arizona) and Pebble (Alaska) as permitting case studies.

Phase 9

Production

Mining is moving rock; processing is getting the metal out of it.

  1. Drill & blast the rock (explosives from Orica, Dyno Nobel).
  2. Load & haul to the crusher (increasingly autonomous trucks).
  3. Crush & grind to powder in SAG and ball mills. This uses huge amounts of power.
  4. Separate: flotation for copper/zinc (bubbles carry mineral to make a ~25–30% copper concentrate); leaching for gold (cyanide, then carbon-in-leach); heap leach for low-grade ore.
  5. Ship: concentrate goes to a smelter (often in China), while gold leaves as a doré bar to a refiner.

Smelters charge TC/RCs (treatment and refining charges), deducted from what the miner is paid. Prices are set off exchanges like the LME and COMEX.

Answer it cold

Read: Freeport-McMoRan 10-K (operations). Komatsu FrontRunner / Caterpillar MineStar pages. A copper flotation plant video.

Phase 10

Closure

Mines end. Companies post reclamation bonds so the government isn't stuck with the cleanup. The biggest long-term risk is tailings, the ground-up waste stored behind dams. Mount Polley (Canada, 2014) and Brumadinho (Brazil, 2019, where about 270 people died) led to the Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management.

Answer it cold

Read: GISTM summary (globaltailingsreview.org). Coverage of the Brumadinho disaster and what changed at Vale.

Boom vs bust

In a metal boom, juniors raise money easily, so drillers and assay labs book out for months and their prices rise. Equipment makers' backlogs grow and royalty/stream companies collect more on fixed contracts. In a bust, junior financing dries up first (the exploration "winter"), so drillers and labs feel it fast, while royalty companies often use the downturn to buy new streams cheaply. Know 2011–2015 (the end of the commodity supercycle) as the case study.
