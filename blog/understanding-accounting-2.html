Understanding Accounting 2 - Assets, liabilities, and equity

Assets, liabilities, and equity. The equation that every recorded transaction keeps in balance, and the reason a bond is a claim, not a chapter of startup folklore.

Series: 1. Language and disclosure · 2. The equation · 3. The statements

What you should be able to do

  • Write the accounting equation, and use it to classify any recorded event as a change in resources, a change in claims, or both.
  • Apply the asset test and the liability test, and name items that fail each test even though they look valuable or look like a promise.
  • Separate current from non-current, and say why the split exists for a lender.
  • Map debit and credit to the equation without treating either word as good or bad.
  • Walk the December consulting firm onto a classified list of assets, liabilities, and equity that still balances.
  • Price a simple bond at a discount, amortize one year of effective interest, and keep the carrying amount a liability the whole time.
  • Tell debt from equity by the claim, not by the word on the term sheet.

The three statements are rearrangements of three piles: assets, liabilities, and equity. Seed, Series A, and an IPO are transactions that hit those piles. Classify the claim first. The statement it lands on comes after the claim is clear.

The equation is a claim identity

Every resource the entity controls is claimed by someone. There is no leftover pile called "the company's own money" sitting beside the claims. If outsiders have a present obligation against those resources, that claim is a liability. Whatever remains is equity. The owners own the residual, not the trucks.

Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Equity

Rearrange it any way that helps you think:

Assets − Liabilities  =  Equity
Assets − Equity       =  Liabilities

The first rearrangement is the net assets identity. Equity is not a pile of cash. It is what is left after the recognized claims of non-owners. The second is less used and still true: if you know the resources and the residual, you know the outsider claims.

The equation is an identity, not a target. It is true after every posted entry because double entry forces it. A set of books that does not balance is not a set of books that is "almost right." An entry was dropped, or an amount was typed once. Find it before you interpret a ratio.

Two mistakes are common. First, treating equity as "what the company is worth." Book equity is the residual of recognized amounts under a named measurement basis. Land bought ten years ago for $1 million still sits at $1 million under historical cost. The market can value the same firm at $8 million. Both numbers can be faithfully represented, and they answer different questions. Second, treating a bank balance as equity. Cash is an asset. The claim on that cash might be a lender's, a customer's (deferred revenue), or the owners'. Read the right-hand side before you spend the left.

Debit and credit are sides, not verdicts

The ledger has a left side and a right side. Debit is left. Credit is right. Which side increases an account depends on where the account sits in the equation.

Account Increase Decrease Normal balance
Asset Debit Credit Debit
Liability Credit Debit Credit
Equity (share capital, retained earnings) Credit Debit Credit
Revenue (closes into equity) Credit Debit Credit
Expense (closes into equity) Debit Credit Debit

Revenue increases equity, so it lives on the credit side. Expense decreases equity, so it lives on the debit side. That is the only reason the income statement has a shape. It is a temporary equity account that we display separately for a period and then close into retained earnings. Booking revenue is already a change in equity, even before anyone prints an income statement.

Contra accounts flip the normal balance of the account they reduce. Accumulated depreciation is a credit sitting against a debit-balance asset. Allowance for doubtful accounts is the same idea against receivables. Treasury stock is a debit sitting against equity. Read the name, then find what it is contra to, before you add it as if it were a new pile.

An asset is a present resource you control

The IFRS Conceptual Framework defines an asset as a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. An economic resource is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits. Potential is enough. The benefit does not have to be certain, and it does not have to be a cash inflow next Tuesday.

Three pieces, and all three have to be there.

  1. Right, with potential benefits. A claim on a customer, a legal title to a machine, a license, a deposit at a bank. A skilled workforce is valuable and usually fails this test, because the entity does not have a right it can enforce against the people walking out. Internally generated goodwill, the excess a buyer would pay for the assembled firm, also fails. You did not acquire a separately identifiable right. Purchased goodwill in a business combination is recognized because a transaction priced the assembled residual. Do not import that treatment onto a firm that was never bought.
  2. Control. The entity can direct the use of the right and obtain the benefits. Legal title is common evidence. It is not the only evidence. A leased warehouse that the lessee controls for the term is the lessee's right-of-use asset, even though the lessor still holds legal title. Inventory sitting at a customer's warehouse on consignment is still the consignor's asset if the consignor still controls it. Possession without control is not an asset of the possessor.
  3. Past event. The right already exists. A purchase order you have issued, with nothing delivered and no payment made, is an executory contract. Both sides still have unperformed promises. Neither an asset nor a liability is recognized for the unperformed part. The machine arriving next month is next month's asset.

Recognition is a second gate. An item can meet the definition and still stay off the statements if it cannot be measured in a way that is useful. Internally generated brands often die at this gate. The advertising spend is real. Isolating a reliable amount for "the brand" usually is not. The spend is expensed. The brand can still be the reason someone buys the shares.

Current versus non-current

The split exists for liquidity. A lender wants to know which resources will turn into cash soon enough to meet the claims that come due soon. Under IAS 1 an asset is current when any one of these is true: you expect to realize it in the normal operating cycle, you hold it primarily for trading, you expect to realize it within twelve months after the reporting date, or it is cash or a cash equivalent unless restricted for at least twelve months. Everything else is non-current.

The operating cycle can be longer than a year. A homebuilder's inventory of houses under construction is current even if the house will not close for eighteen months, because that is the cycle. A bond you intend to hold to a maturity thirteen months away is non-current, even though it is a financial instrument, unless you have classified it as current for another reason. Intent and the calendar both matter. Read the classification policy before you build a current ratio.

US GAAP uses a similar current and non-current presentation for most non-financial companies. The tests are close enough to treat as one idea, until you open a bank or a broker. There the operating cycle is the whole book, the statement is often unclassified, and the current split is less useful. The examples here stay with a commercial or industrial presentation.

The asset classes you will actually meet

Item What the right is Usual measurement The trap
Cash and cash equivalents Demand deposits and short, highly liquid instruments that convert to known amounts of cash and face an insignificant risk of change in value. IFRS uses a three-month remaining-maturity idea for equivalents. Face. Foreign currency is translated at the closing rate. A compensating balance that cannot be used is restricted cash. It may still be cash, and it is not cash you can spend. A line of credit you have not drawn is not cash.
Accounts receivable The contractual right to cash from a customer for goods or services already transferred. Amortized cost, reduced by expected credit losses. IFRS 9 is a forward-looking expected-loss model. Older US language of "probable incurred losses" has been replaced by CECL, which is also expected-loss, with a different construction. Gross receivables without the allowance overstate the asset. The allowance is a contra-asset, not a liability.
Inventory Goods held for sale, in production, or consumed in production. Lower of cost and net realizable value. Cost can be specific identification, FIFO, or a weighted average. US GAAP still allows LIFO. IFRS does not. A write-down is a new cost basis under US GAAP for most inventory. IFRS can reverse a write-down if NRV recovers, limited to the amount previously written down. The same warehouse can show two different recoveries.
Prepaid expenses A payment that buys a future benefit you have not yet consumed. Insurance, rent paid ahead. Unconsumed cost. Prepaying does not create an expense. Consuming creates the expense. The December insurance payment is this case: $12,000 paid, $1,000 consumed.
Property, plant, and equipment Tangible items held for use in production, supply, rental, or administration, expected to be used for more than one period. Cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment. IFRS also allows a revaluation model. US GAAP does not revalue PPE upward. Land is not depreciated. A building on the land is. The residual value is an estimate, and changing it is a change in estimate, not an error.
Intangible assets An identifiable non-monetary asset without physical substance. Identifiable means it is separable or arises from a contract or law. Cost less amortization and impairment, if the life is finite. Indefinite-lived intangibles are not amortized. They are tested for impairment. Research is expensed. IFRS can capitalize development after a list of tests. US GAAP expenses most R&D. Same engineers, different asset.
Financial assets other than receivables A contractual right to cash, or an equity instrument of another entity. Depends on the business model and the cash-flow test: amortized cost, fair value through OCI, or fair value through profit or loss. US GAAP has its own classification map. Do not mix the maps. Fair value of a holding is not "the company made that much cash." The gain can sit in OCI and never have been collected.

Impairment is the admission that the asset will not recover its carrying amount. For PPE and finite-lived intangibles, IFRS compares carrying amount to recoverable amount, the higher of value in use and fair value less costs of disposal, and can reverse a later recovery (not for goodwill). US GAAP uses a two-step idea for long-lived assets to be held and used: first an recoverability test on undiscounted cash flows, then a write-down to fair value if that test fails. A reversal is generally prohibited. The same factory can be unimpaired under US GAAP, because undiscounted cash flows still cover the book, and impaired under IFRS, because the discounted value in use does not. Measurement is a choice the framework already made.

A liability is a present obligation you cannot avoid

The IFRS Conceptual Framework defines a liability as a present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events. An obligation is a duty or responsibility that the entity has no practical ability to avoid.

Three pieces again.

  1. Present obligation. It exists now. A board plan to buy a machine next year is not an obligation. A signed purchase you can cancel without penalty is not one either. A constructive obligation can exist without a contract if past practice has created a valid expectation in the counterparty that the entity will pay. Warranties and restructuring announcements fail or pass on that test, not on whether a lawyer drafted a note.
  2. Transfer of a resource. Cash, goods, services, or another asset. An obligation to issue a fixed number of your own shares is often equity, not a liability, because your own shares are not an asset of the entity. An obligation to issue a variable number of shares worth a fixed dollar amount is often a liability, because the economic substance is "we owe $X," settled in paper. The word "shares" on the term sheet does not decide the classification. The variability does.
  3. Past event. The obligating event has already happened. Goods were received. Work was done. A law was broken. A customer paid you in advance, and you now owe the performance. The December customer deposit of $20,000 is this last case.

A provision is a liability of uncertain timing or amount. Recognize it when the obligation is present, an outflow is probable, and the amount can be estimated reliably. Probable is not the same bar in the two books. IFRS recognizes a provision when an outflow is more likely than not. US GAAP recognizes a loss contingency when the loss is probable, meaning likely, and the amount can be estimated. The same lawsuit can be a liability under IFRS and only a paragraph in the notes under US GAAP. A contingent liability that fails recognition still has to be disclosed unless the outflow is remote. Equity investors who only read the totals miss the case that is sitting in the notes.

An executory contract is still not a liability for the unperformed part. A five-year services agreement you signed yesterday, with nothing delivered and nothing paid, does not put five years of fees on the balance sheet. A lease is no longer treated that way for the lessee. IFRS 16 and US GAAP Topic 842 put a right-of-use asset and a lease liability on the books for most leases. The old operating-lease off-balance-sheet treatment is the historical exam trick, not the current rule for public companies. Private-company elections can still look more like the old world. Read the policy note.

Current versus non-current claims

A liability is current when you expect to settle it in the operating cycle, you hold it for trading, it is due within twelve months, or you do not have the right at the reporting date to defer settlement for at least twelve months. The last test is the one that changes the classification of a long-term loan.

A five-year loan with a remaining life of six months is current, even if you intend to refinance it, unless at the reporting date you already have a right to refinance or to roll it for at least twelve months. IFRS looks at rights existing at the reporting date. A refinance completed after the reporting date, before the statements are issued, does not turn the loan non-current under IFRS. US GAAP can treat a post-balance-sheet refinance as evidence that the loan is non-current if the refinance is completed before issuance. Same loan, same January refinance, two classifications. The current ratio moves. The cash does not.

Covenant breaches do the same damage. If a long-term facility becomes callable because you missed a ratio, the whole facility is current unless you have obtained a waiver that gives you the right to defer for twelve months, and that waiver exists at the date the standard requires. Do not classify a breached loan as non-current because "the bank likes us."

Equity is the residual claim

Equity is Assets minus Liabilities. That is the definition. Everything else is a subdivision of the residual for users who want to know how the residual got there and who owns it.

Piece What it records
Share capital, or common stock at par The legal capital of the shares issued. Some jurisdictions have no par. Then the whole proceeds sit in one capital account.
Additional paid-in capital, or share premium Proceeds above par. The economic contribution of the owners at issuance, minus the par slice.
Retained earnings Cumulative profit not distributed. This is earned equity, not contributed. A firm can have large retained earnings and no cash. Profit was earned and then tied up in receivables or machines.
Accumulated other comprehensive income Gains and losses that the framework keeps out of profit: certain foreign-currency translations, certain fair-value swings, certain pension remeasurements. ASPE has no OCI bucket. The same gain can sit in profit under ASPE.
Treasury stock The entity's own shares reacquired. A contra-equity. You do not recognize a gain on trading your own shares.
Non-controlling interest The residual that belongs to outside owners of a subsidiary, presented inside equity on the consolidated statements. It is equity of the group, not of the parent's shareholders.

Contributed capital is what owners put in. Earned capital is what the entity kept. A dividend is a distribution of earned capital, not an expense. It never hits profit. It reduces retained earnings and reduces cash, or it creates a payable. Share issuances increase contributed capital. They are not income. An IPO is a share issuance. It is not revenue.

Preferred shares are the first hybrid. If the shares are perpetual, dividends are discretionary, and there is no obligation to deliver cash, they are equity. If the shares are mandatorily redeemable for cash on a date, they are a liability, no matter what the certificate says. If dividends are cumulative and still discretionary, missed dividends are not a liability until the entity has an obligation it cannot avoid. Disclosure of arrears is not recognition. Read the redemption clause before you put preferred in equity because the name has "share" in it.

The December firm, classified

The December firm has four leftovers and an $85,000 accrual profit. To put them on the equation, start from an opening position.

On November 30 the firm has $50,000 of cash, and no other assets or claims. The founder contributed that cash for common shares. Share capital is $50,000. Retained earnings are $0.

Work of $100,000 is delivered and invoiced, unpaid. A different customer pays a $20,000 deposit for January work. Rent of $8,000 is paid. On December 1 the firm pays $12,000 for twelve months of insurance. Wages of $6,000 are earned and unpaid.

Walk each event through the equation. If a row does not balance, stop. The event was not recorded.

Event Assets Liabilities Equity
November 30 Cash 50,000 — Share capital 50,000
Pay insurance Cash −12,000; Prepaid +12,000 — —
Pay rent Cash −8,000 — Rent expense −8,000
Deliver and invoice Receivable +100,000 — Revenue +100,000
Collect the deposit Cash +20,000 Deferred revenue +20,000 —
Accrue wages — Wages payable +6,000 Wage expense −6,000
Consume one month of insurance Prepaid −1,000 — Insurance expense −1,000

December 31, classified:

Amount
Cash 50,000
Accounts receivable 100,000
Prepaid insurance 11,000
Current assets 161,000
Non-current assets —
Total assets 161,000
Wages payable 6,000
Deferred revenue 20,000
Current liabilities 26,000
Share capital 50,000
Retained earnings 85,000
Equity 135,000
Liabilities + equity 161,000

Cash did not move, net, across December. 50,000 in, 50,000 out, 20,000 back. Profit is 85,000. Net assets rose by 85,000, from 50,000 to 135,000. That rise is the receivable, the remaining prepaid, and the two new claims. Equity rose because revenue exceeded expenses, not because the bank account grew. A current ratio of 161 / 26 looks comfortable. Eleven thousand of that numerator is insurance, not cash, and 100,000 of it has not been collected. The equation is balanced. Liquidity is a different question, and the cash flow statement is where it shows up.

Prepaid insurance of 11,000 is current if the remaining eleven months all fall inside the next year, which they do. If the policy had been three years, you would split the prepaid: the next twelve months current, the rest non-current. The whole prepaid does not go in current just because it is not a building. The calendar, not the name, decides.

Debt is a liability with a contract

Debt is not a fourth element. It is a liability whose terms usually include a principal, a cost of borrowing, and a maturity. Bank loans, notes, bonds, commercial paper, and most convertible notes sit here. Trade payables are liabilities and are not what people mean by debt. Deferred revenue is a liability and is not debt. When a credit agreement defines Debt with a capital D, read the definition. It often includes leases and sometimes excludes revolving amounts you have not drawn. The accounting total and the covenant total are cousins, not twins.

Interest is the cost of having the claim outstanding. Under the effective-interest method, interest expense for a period is the carrying amount at the start of the period times the market yield at issuance. The cash coupon can be higher or lower. The difference amortizes the premium or the discount. Cash-basis "interest I paid" is not the expense. Expense the coupon and ignore the amortization, and you report the wrong profit every year of the bond's life, and the wrong liability on every balance sheet after issuance.

Secured debt has a specific claim on identified assets. Unsecured debt has a general claim. Subordinated debt stands behind senior debt in a liquidation. None of that changes the fact that all of them are liabilities. It changes the recovery a particular lender should expect, and it belongs in the notes and in the debt agreement. Priority is not classification.

A bond is a packaged debt claim

A bond is a transferable package of the same claim. The indenture sets the face amount, the coupon, the payment dates, the maturity, the covenants, and any call or conversion features. The issuer writes the coupon. The market writes the yield. Those two rates are equal only by accident, on the day the bond is priced at par.

If at issuance The cash the issuer receives The liability at issuance
Coupon equals yield Face Face. Par.
Coupon below yield Less than face Less than face. Discount. Investors demanded a cheaper price so their return equals the market rate.
Coupon above yield More than face More than face. Premium. Investors paid up for a coupon richer than the market.

The liability is the present value of the remaining contractual cash flows, discounted at the market rate on the issue date. Subsequent accounting for a vanilla instrument held at amortized cost does not update that discount rate each period. Fair-value accounting, if elected or required, does. Know which model you are in before you ask why the liability did not move when yields moved.

Worked issuance

The firm later issues a two-year bond. Face $100,000. Annual coupon 10 percent, paid at each anniversary. The market yield for this credit on the issue date is 12 percent. No transaction costs, so the issue price equals the present value of the cash flows at 12 percent.

PV of face      = 100,000 / 1.12²           = 79,719
PV of coupons   = 10,000 / 1.12
                  + 10,000 / 1.12²          = 16,901
Issue price                                 = 96,620
Debit  Cash                    96,620
Credit Bonds payable                    96,620

Some ledgers keep Bonds payable at face and a separate Discount on bonds payable of 3,380. The net carrying amount is still 96,620. Net presentation and gross presentation are the same liability. Use one and stay with it.

Year 1, effective interest:

Interest expense  = 96,620 × 0.12  = 11,594
Cash coupon                        = 10,000
Amortization of discount           =  1,594
Carrying amount at anniversary     = 98,214
Debit  Interest expense        11,594
Credit Cash                             10,000
Credit Bonds payable                     1,594

Year 2:

Interest expense  = 98,214 × 0.12  = 11,786
Cash coupon                        = 10,000
Amortization                       =  1,786
Carrying amount at maturity        = 100,000

The last year's effective rate is held so the carrying amount lands on face. Rounding of a dollar is normal. What is not normal is expensing 10,000 each year and leaving the liability at 96,620 until maturity, then booking a 3,380 loss at redemption. That path reports the wrong expense in both years and a phantom loss at the end. The economics were a 12 percent borrowing from the first day.

At maturity:

Debit  Bonds payable          100,000
Credit Cash                            100,000

The 100,000 is not an expense. The claim is being settled. Interest was already recognized along the way. Debiting interest expense for the redemption mixes the settlement of the claim with the cost of carrying it.

Between issuance and the first anniversary the classification follows the remaining life. On the issue date the whole 96,620 is non-current. On the balance sheet one year before maturity, the remaining carrying amount is current, unless you already have a refinance right that meets the standard. Interest payable, if the coupon date is not the reporting date, is a separate current liability for the accrued slice.

Features that change the claim, not the element

  • Callable. The issuer can redeem early, usually at a premium. The call is an option of the issuer. Under amortized-cost accounting you still start from the cash flows you expect. A call does not make the bond equity.
  • Convertible into a fixed number of shares. IFRS splits a vanilla convertible into a liability equal to the value of a straight bond and an equity component for the residual. US GAAP often keeps a conventional convertible as a single liability, with exceptions for cash-conversion features and certain beneficial conversion features. The same instrument can be one line or two lines depending on the book. Conversion, when it happens, swaps a liability for equity. It does not create a gain because you "retired cheap debt."
  • Convertible into a variable number of shares worth a fixed amount. This is a debt claim being settled in a variable number of shares. It is usually a liability in whole. The share settlement is a form of payment, not a contribution of capital at issuance.

Zero-coupon bonds are the discount case with a coupon of zero. The entire return is amortization. Deep-discount munis and stripped Treasuries are this idea in the market. The accounting is the same effective-interest engine.

Debt versus equity is a claim test

Put the two residual questions next to each other. A liability answers "what must we transfer, and can we avoid it?" Equity answers "what is left?" Hybrid paper tries to look like one and behave like the other. The framework does not care what the bankers called the round.

Debt Equity
Obligation to deliver cash or another asset Yes, or to settle in a variable number of shares worth a fixed amount. No, if the only settlement is a residual interest in a fixed number of shares, and dividends are discretionary.
Return to the holder Interest, a contractual yield. Miss it and you are in default. Dividends, if declared. Miss them and you have disappointed people. You have not defaulted, unless a separate contract says otherwise.
Maturity Usually. Perpetual debt exists, and the other tests still have to be run. Usually none. Mandatorily redeemable stock is the exception, and it is a liability.
Priority Ahead of residual owners. Last. Preferred sits ahead of common inside equity, or hops out of equity if it is really debt.
Effect of issuance on profit None at issuance. Interest later reduces profit. None at issuance. Dividends never reduce profit.
Effect of issuance on cash Financing inflow, then financing outflows for interest and principal. Interest paid can be operating or financing on the cash flow statement, depending on the framework. The claim is a liability either way. Financing inflow. Distributions are financing outflows.

A founder loan that has no interest, no maturity, and a history of never being called can still be a liability if the company cannot avoid repayment when the founder asks. Substance is not "we all know I will not take the money out." Substance is whether the entity has a practical ability to refuse. Document the thing you mean. If you mean equity, issue shares or formally convert the loan. Leaving a related-party payable on the books and calling it "patient capital" is how a due-diligence file later reclassifies your equity ratio in a week.

A SAFE, a convertible note, and a priced preferred share can land in three different places on the right-hand side. Run the liability test before you name the account. Once the claim is a liability or equity, the cash flow line is the financing section, or a non-cash disclosure if no cash moved.

What to hold

Assets are present resources you control from past events. Liabilities are present obligations you cannot avoid. Equity is the residual. Debt is a liability with a contract. A bond is that contract made transferable, measured at the present value of its remaining cash flows, and kept at amortized cost unless a fair-value model applies. Debits and credits are how the piles move. They are not moral language.

The December firm has 161,000 of assets, 26,000 of liabilities, and 135,000 of equity. Profit for the month is 85,000. Cash is unchanged. The balance sheet is that identity at a date. The income statement is the change in the residual from operations. The cash flow statement is the change in the cash line. A seed round, a Series A, and an IPO are transactions that hit these piles. They are not a fourth kind of accounting.
