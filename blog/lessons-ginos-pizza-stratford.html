Lessons from running a pizza franchise with my family while in high school

Our family ran a Gino's Pizza franchise in Stratford, Ontario, from 2010 to 2020. I worked it through high school. These are the lessons the store taught me before I ever called anything a startup.

Stratford during the Festival was chaos. Tourists everywhere. My mom, dad, and brother were at the store every single day. Not as a schedule they liked. As the schedule that was. Five years without a day off—not one. Christmas was it. One day, rotated so someone was always there.

I was in high school. My job was to show up when I could. Lunch period. My spare after. Ten-minute walk from school to the pizza shop. I'd work the counter, feed the kids who came to see the plays, then head back to class or home. By 4 or 5, the rush would start. My brother and I would come back and work until 10 or 11. Weekends we'd go until 10, then party with friends until 1 a.m., exhausted but not stopping.

My brother was the one grinding. He'd close the shop—1 a.m., sometimes later. Night after night. My parents did mornings. Me and my brother did nights. Friday and Saturday we'd all be there in the evenings, my brother staying until the last customer left. That's what it took.

What I watched them build

Slowly, it worked. Sales went up. The family was climbing. All that labor, all those days with no break—it was adding up. Not because the brand was good (though it was), but because they wouldn't stop.

Then COVID hit. They decided. Time to go. My parents sold it. Ten years. Done.

What a pizza shop teaches you

Every single night, the cash drawer either covered the week or it didn't. There was no story that fixed it. No growth narrative. No excuses. The money was either there or it wasn't.

I learned to watch the numbers. Food costs. Labor costs. What the franchise took. Everything tiny adds up. A percentage here, a percentage there, and suddenly your margin is gone. You can have a packed night and still lose money. Royalties come off the top. Employees get paid whether it was busy or slow. The kitchen still consumes food. A good night isn't about volume. It's about whether you're actually making money.

My parents knew this. They tracked it obsessively. Every penny. Every expense. They'd sit and calculate what stayed. Nothing was guessed. Everything was counted.

The numbers tell the story

Say a week does $10,000 in sales. That sounds good until you see the breakdown:

  • Food costs: $5,000
  • Labor: $3,000
  • Rent: $2,000

You're left with $0. Margin gone. And that's before utilities, supplies, franchise royalties, or anything else.

That's when you realize: most of your expenses don't move. Rent is due every month whether you're busy or dead. Payroll is the same. Food costs scale with volume, but not linearly—you still need people there at 5 a.m. prepping, whether you sell 50 pizzas or 500.

Lesson one: Control expenses

This is where my parents lived. Every single line item. Food waste. Labor hours. Whether you actually needed someone on the clock at that moment. They'd calculate: if we cut this, what breaks? If we spend here, what do we gain?

It's not about being cheap. It's about knowing that a 5% food waste is money gone. That paying someone for 8 hours when you only need 6 costs you. That the phone bill is $50 and that's 5 pizzas you didn't make profit on.

Most small business owners don't track this obsessively. My parents did. It's exhausting and boring and it's exactly what keeps you alive.

Lesson two: Increase sales

But controlling expenses only gets you to break-even. To actually make money, you need to push sales.

Every $1,000 in additional weekly sales—when your fixed costs are already covered—drops almost entirely to the bottom line. Food costs scale up, sure. But rent doesn't. Labor is mostly there anyway. That extra $1,000 becomes $700, $800 in profit.

That's why my parents built relationships with schools, community groups, employers nearby. They'd sponsor events. They'd deliver to local offices for lunch. They'd make sure the shop was part of Stratford, not just a location on a street. When the Festival hit, they weren't competing on pizza alone—they had a reputation.

Sales growth is the only real leverage in a small business. It's also the hardest thing to engineer. You can control costs by cutting. You can't cut your way to profit. You have to sell more.

Lesson three: Hire carefully

We went through a string of bad hires. People who didn't show up. People who needed constant managing. People who slowed down the operation instead of speeding it up.

A bad employee doesn't just cost you their wage. They cost management time. They cost reliability. You can't trust them to be there, so you have to be there. You have to train them over and over. You have to cover for them. A bad hire can be worth more headache than actual help.

My parents learned to be ruthless about this. You don't hire someone and hope they work out. You hire carefully, or you don't hire at all. Because the wrong person on the team multiplies your work, not divides it.

What this actually means

Running a small business is: obsessively tracking where every dollar goes, cutting everything that doesn't serve customers, then working like hell to bring in more customers. All of it. Every single day. For years.

You're 24/7 mentally. Maybe not physically in the store, but mentally you never leave. The spreadsheet is in your head. The staffing problem is in your head. Whether you hit margin this week is in your head.

Most people see the revenue number and think that's profit. They don't see the spreadsheet. They don't see my parents doing inventory at midnight. They don't see my brother figuring out which shift he can skip to save labor costs, then deciding he can't because customers need him there.

The honest truth

Small business and side hustle is the most glamorous thing on the internet right now. Everyone talks about it. Be your own boss. Build your thing.

I can tell you from firsthand experience: it's not easy. And if I had a gun held to my head, I would never get back into the food industry or a franchise restaurant again.

The problem isn't that it doesn't work. It's the input-to-output ratio. You put in an insane amount of time and stress and mental energy. It's 24/7 mentally, maybe not physically, but you never really leave it. What comes out is money. Slow. But guaranteed, if you show up.

The output is almost guaranteed if you do put in the time. You show up, you work, you will make money. Slowly. Surely. But it's not easy and it's not quick. The value you get out compared to the input is just... it's low. Really low. Even when it's working.

That's the real lesson. Not the romantic internet version.
