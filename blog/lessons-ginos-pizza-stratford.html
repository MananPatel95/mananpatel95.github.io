Lessons from running a pizza franchise with my family while in high school

Our family ran a Gino's Pizza franchise in Stratford, Ontario, from 2010 to 2020. I worked it through high school. These are the lessons the store taught me before I ever called anything a startup.

Stratford is a small city. In summer the Festival fills it. In January it is quiet, cold, and local. For ten years our family business was a Gino's on that cycle—packed during festival season, lean in winter. I worked there through high school. Class, then a shift. A test the next morning did not pause the dinner rush.

A franchise is a strange first business. You do not invent the pizza. You buy the right to make someone else's recipe, under their name, with their suppliers and their rules. Customers call it yours. So does the rent. So does the night the driver does not show.

1. The brand is rented. The shift is owned.

Gino's gave us a recipe, a look, and a phone number people already trusted. You skip the years of building belief. That is the point of a franchise.

What the brand does not do is run Friday. Someone still has to be on the make line during rush, answer phones when an order is wrong, and count the drawer when the lights go off. Royalties come off the top whether the night was good or bad. The fee is the cost of the name. Everything else is the operator's problem.

I learned early to separate them. Loving the logo is not the job. Making the same pizza the same way on a tired Tuesday is the job.

2. Family is the org chart

There was no cofounder search. The people in the store were the people at home. That is a gift and a trap.

The gift is hours. A family will cover a shift that a normal schedule cannot. Trust is already there. You do not onboard your own house. When someone is sick, the bench is the kitchen table.

The trap is that roles blur. I was a high school kid and I was also labor. A disagreement about a close is a disagreement you take home. You cannot write a performance review for your family and then eat dinner. The store works when everyone knows which hat they are wearing in the shop, and takes that hat off at the door. We were better on the nights we did that.

3. High school does not pause the dinner rush

School wanted the evening. The store wanted the evening. The store won more often than my homework wanted it to.

That sounds like a complaint. It was a schedule. Customers order at dinner, not at a time that fits a timetable. If you are the person on the schedule, you are the promise that the pizza shows up. I learned to treat a shift like a commitment with someone else's name on it. You can be sixteen and still be the reason a family eats.

It also taught me the limit. A person who only works and never sleeps becomes a bad employee and a bad student. The weeks that worked were the weeks the schedule was honest about both.

4. Stratford has two stores in one year

From spring through the Festival, the town swells. Visitors eat out. Locals are busy. The phone is a different phone.

Then the theatres go dark and it is a Stratford winter. Same address. Fewer tickets. The people who order are the people who live there, and they remember if you were careless in July.

You staff the week you are in. Carrying a summer crew into January burns wages. Carrying a January crew into festival season burns customers. A small city does not give you a national average to hide behind. The week is the forecast.

5. The pizza is a cost problem wearing cheese

Revenue is the easy number. The box going out the door. The hard numbers are what it took to build the box.

  • Food. Dough, cheese, toppings. Cheese moves in price. If the portion stays generous and the menu stays still, the margin leaves quietly.
  • Labor. Every pie is minutes. A rushed night with too few people makes bad pizza. A slow night with too many people makes a good pizza you cannot afford.
  • The franchise take. Royalties and advertising are a percent of sales, not a percent of profit. A busy night can still be a thin night after the name is paid for.

I did not have the words "contribution margin" in grade eleven. I had a drawer, a stack of boxes, and a sense of whether the night had been worth it. That sense was usually right, and it was never a substitute for actually counting.

6. Cash is nightly

A pizza shop does not wait for a quarterly close to find out if it is alive. Suppliers want to be paid. Staff want to be paid. Rent wants to be paid. The till either covered the week or it did not.

That is the lesson I keep. Later, building software, it was easy to talk about growth and forget the drawer. The Stratford store did not allow that. If the money was not there on a Thursday, there was no story that made Thursday fine. Cashflow is not a slogan. It is whether you can buy cheese on Friday.

7. Ten years is a chapter, not a personality

2010 to 2020 is a long time to be one store. I started as a high school kid in the shop. The franchise was still the family business after high school was over. A place like that starts to feel like the permanent shape of your life, because it was the shape of the week.

It was not permanent. The store had a first year and a last year. I am glad we ran it. I am also glad I can see it as a business with a term, not as the only thing I was allowed to be. The skills stayed. The address did not have to.

What stayed with me

Show up when scheduled. Know what the name costs. Watch food and labor before celebrating a busy night. Keep family roles clear. Count the drawer.

A decade of pizza in Stratford was my first operating job. Everything since has been easier to romanticize and worse when I forgot the drawer.
