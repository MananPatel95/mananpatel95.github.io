Autonomy City: learning how self-driving cars see

I wanted to actually understand what a self-driving car sees, not just read about it. So I built Autonomy City: a small 3D city in the browser where one car drives itself, and you can flip between three different ways of seeing the street.

▶ Play Autonomy City Runs in your browser. Best on a laptop or desktop with a keyboard.

It's built with TypeScript and Three.js, with the lidar ray casting in Rust compiled to WebAssembly. Behind the game there's a Python data pipeline that trains small models on what the cars record. Everything here is a simplified stand-in based on what these companies say in public. It's not their software, and none of them are involved.

Three ways to see the same street

The fun part is that F1, F2, and F3 aren't just different overlays. Each one has its own planner, so the car actually drives differently depending on what it's allowed to see.

F1: cameras only (Tesla-style)

Tesla says it doesn't need lidar or radar, and doesn't rely on detailed HD maps. So in this view the car only gets cameras. It draws boxes around what it sees, builds a rough occupancy picture from that, and follows the nav route like you'd follow Google Maps.

What clicked for me: if a camera can't see it, it doesn't exist. Hide a pedestrian behind a parked car and this stack finds out late.

F2: lidar, radar, and an HD map (Waymo-style)

Waymo's 6th-gen Driver uses 13 cameras, 4 lidars, and 6 radars, and only drives in areas it has mapped in detail. In the sim, a spinning lidar sweeps 360° around the car, radar pings show up, and everything gets fused into one set of boxes. The car drives on a pre-built lane graph instead of reading the road live.

That motion fix was a real lesson. A lidar takes time to spin, and the car moves during that spin. If you don't correct for it, a pole looks bent.

F3: occupancy forecasting (Waabi-style)

Waabi describes an "AI-first" stack that stays interpretable, trained heavily in its own simulator. Here the car turns lidar into a bird's-eye grid, forecasts that grid 1 to 3 seconds out, and scores a fan of possible paths against the forecast. It also runs variants of the same scene, like a jaywalker stepping out a bit earlier, to see where the plan breaks.

The part nobody sees: the data engine

Most of the work in self-driving isn't the car. It's the loop that makes the car better. So I built a small version of that:

  • Record drives, in the browser or headless in Node.
  • Mine the interesting moments: takeovers, perception misses, near-misses.
  • Label them with a fake labeling vendor that adds noise on purpose, then QA to catch the bad labels.
  • Split by scenario so the test set isn't just the training set again.
  • Train a PointNet-style perception model and a driving policy with behaviour cloning and DAgger in PyTorch.
  • Gate the new model: it only ships if it beats the old one. Then export to ONNX and run it in the browser.

The perception model hit an F1 of 0.99 against 0.62 for the hand-written baseline. I want to be honest about that number though. It's on simulated data with clean geometry, and the test objects were all close to the car. Real roads are much harder. The point was to learn the loop, not to set a record.

What I took away

  • Sensors decide what's possible. The planner can only be as good as what it's allowed to see.
  • Maps are a trade. An HD map makes driving easier, but you're stuck where you've mapped.
  • Prediction is the hard part. Seeing a pedestrian is easy. Knowing whether they'll step out is the real problem.
  • The data loop is the product. Mining, labelling, QA, and gating matter more than any one model.

It's the same thing I see building LLM agents at work. The model is only part of it. Evals, data, and knowing when something is safe to ship are the rest.
